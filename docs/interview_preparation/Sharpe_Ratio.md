The Sharpe Ratio measures the risk-adjusted return of an investment asset or a trading portfolio. It calculates how much excess return you receive for the extra volatility you endure by holding a riskier asset instead of a risk-free investment. [1, 2, 3, 4, 5] 
## How it Works
The Sharpe Ratio tells you if a portfolio's high returns are due to smart investment decisions or a result of taking on too much dangerous risk. [6, 7] 

* Formula: $\text{Sharpe Ratio} = \frac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$
* $R_p$: Return of the portfolio or stock
   * $R_f$: Risk-free rate of return (e.g., U.S. Treasury Bond yield)
   * $\sigma_p$: Standard deviation of the portfolio's excess return (volatility) [8, 9, 10, 11, 12] 

## Grading the Sharpe Ratio
A higher Sharpe Ratio is always better because it means you are getting more return per unit of risk. [13, 14] 

* Under 1.0: Sub-optimal or poor.
* 1.0 to 1.99: Good.
* 2.0 to 2.99: Very good.
* 3.0 or higher: Excellent. [15, 16, 17, 18, 19] 

## How It Connects to Your Trading Metrics

* P/L Ratio vs. Sharpe Ratio: Your P/L ratio and win rate look at individual trade targets. The Sharpe Ratio looks at the bigger picture of your entire equity curve over months or years to see how smoothly your account is growing. [20, 21] 
* Filtering RSI Strategies: If you test an RSI trading strategy that yields a 3:1 P/L ratio, it might look great on paper. However, if the strategy suffers massive, wild drops in account balance before hitting those profit targets, it will have a low Sharpe Ratio, indicating a bumpy and high-risk ride. [22, 23, 24]
